Mount Temple

Mount Temple

August 19th, 2026

Lake Louise, Banff National Park, Alberta

3543m

Out of the 58 11000er peaks in the Canadian rockies Mt Temple is well known as being the most acessible and least technical. While it has quite an intimidating North Face when viewed from Moraine Lake, the standard route goes up a ridge along the southwest side with only moderate class 3 scrambling and quite a LOT of scree. Due to it’s moderate difficulty, it’s commonly done in a day trip starting from Moraine Lake, hiking up to Larch Valley, and then scrambling up the summit massif.

My family and I were going to be camping at the Lake Louise campground, and upon doing some research on the nearby peaks before our trip to Banff, I was quickly intrigued by Mount Temple. The nature of the approach meant that while I was bagging the peak, my family could enjoy a trip around both Moraine and Louise lakes without me.

Access to Moraine lake is restricted to Parks Canada and commercial shuttles, so I had to wake up at 7am in the morning to book them. It took me about 20 minutes of clicking and refreshing before I was finally able to get spots for us, but I was glad that it was out of the way.

Waking up at the Lake Louise campsite groggy in the cold, we quickly hopped in the car to drive to the shuttle pickup at the Lake Louise ski lodge. After a little bit of confusion, I was able to get onto the bus to Moraine Lake. I arrived at the Lake around 9am, and quickly snagged a beautiful view of Mt Temple, my objective

After some meanderings at the Lake I found the trail leading up to Larch valley. The sun came up and I started quickly to grind out the switchbacks going up. The grade wasn’t too bad, but the best part was how well the trail was kept. Compared to logging road at Lions bay, this was heaven.

After around an hour or so of heading up the grade finally eased out. Now, I was officially in the Larch valley area and I could see the summit massif again. From here, I started seeing alot more people hiking. I still wasn’t out of treeline yet, but I could see scattered alpine meadows here and there as I walked forward. I could also hear alot of chipmunks chirping about.

After 30 minutes or so of hiking, I finally had left treeline. I could start seeing the unobscured valley in front of me aswell as the “sentinel pass” that leads up to the scramble route for Temple.

Further hiking through the valley gave me some really crazy views. It was awesome being surrounded by such rocky.. heh and prominent peaks on all sides. Eventually, I reached the minstemma lakes. While not the same vibrant color as Moraine or Louise, they had a special greenish tint that was pretty cute. The sound of the creek gurling between them was also a nice break in the environment.

From the lakes I was finally able to see sentinel pass. LOTS of others were on the way up/down and the trail looked really crowded. I was excited to finally be able to start some scrambling. I pushed forward and struggled up the trail, wanting to give myself a big rest once I reached the top.

Around 20 minutes of trudging later, I made it to the top. For the normal trail through Larch valley, this was the end, offering a great view of Larch valley behind us, and Paradise ahead. There were lots of people up here, but I didn’t see anyone heading down do Paradise valley or starting the scramble up to Mt Temple. I was feeling a pee, so I ducked behind a rock near the start of the climb to do my business. I had brought lunch up (rice and broiled beef) but wasn’t feeling particularly hungry. I was making good time, and it only took me 1.5 hours to make it to this point. I was getting excited as I looked at the climb ahead, and decided to continue without taking a substantial break. I layered my windjacket over my fleece and put my helmet on as I started the scramble.

Finally starting the scramble and getting my hands on some rock felt good after the arduous hike up. Aura farmed a little bit by popping out behind the rock with my helmet up and heading up climber’s right. It was also nice leaving the busy, tourist trail behind for some nice silent scrambling. The start of the climb consisted of 10-15 minutes of class 2, before transitioning into a walk up some 30 degree scree. This was my jacket’s first time out in a technical field, and it was holding up really well despite me scraping it against alot of rocks. My previous climbs were all in the cascades, and their volcanic nature forms sharp, solid rock giving alot of great holds and solid areas. The rockies are sedimentary, and suprised me how much different the climb was. From afar, the mountains looked like layer cakes compared to the more “mountain” mountains near the coast. The rock quality of the holds were not great, and many of the slopes of the mountain were comrpised of gravel and scree, which had to be avoideds by going on narrow rock bands. When I was on the scree, there was a decently well marked trail that switchbacked up helping to ease the grade. Some of the parts were still very slippery and I needed to pay special attention even when I wasn’t scrambling. As I trekked on, some other cool formations also came into view.

Past the initial scramble from the ridge, I pushed up the screen slope past the big rock pillar checkpoint. When I was further down, I saw some climbers near this formation but as I got around I couldn’t see them. I was going quite quick, and was confident that I would be able to catch up to them. It was quite the “typical” terrain from here, with more of the loose formations that I was getting used to in the rocky mountains. Personally, I like this better than never ending hiking in treeline. It’s alot better being able to see the exposure and views around you all the time vs. being stuck in the woods. However, there wasn’t alot of good spots to take breaks due to all of the loose rock.

In addition to looking like layer cakes, the sedimentary Rocky mountains are also distinguished by their gently parabolic bottoms. These slopes are made completely out of debris from previous rockslides, and while the route going up mainly follows rockbands (solid rock structures in the “sea” of scree) hiking up these slopes were a pain in the ass.

I traversed along the side of the pillar and continued hiking up the loose rock. At a little ledge, I encountered 3 other climbers and passed them. The reason why I was so intent on passing others was to minimize the risk of rockfall. 3 others above me would be a huge danger to myself (3x rockfall risk), while me being above them would be much less dangerous. The ledge was also a nice place to slow down my pace and relax a little bit before continuing up.

Trekking up this screen ledge and between 2 more cool rock formations, I was greeted by the second rock band to scramble up. The first rock band was the scramble at the start of the climb, and everything in between was just loose rock until this second rock band. This is what makes up the “layer cake” mountain formations of the rockies, just alternating scree seas and rock bands. It was a nice change to get my hands back on the rock and start scrambling. The scrambling was class 2-3, and I didn’t feel particularly exposed.

At the top of the slab, I went off route by accident (everything looked the same) due to me going a little more right than I was supposed to be. This off route part split me off into a different section of the scramble (regular route would’ve just avoided this techincal part entirely) This part was a lot harder than the slab scramble I just did (couldn’t take any photos) so I had to focus up. Good thing I was off route as I ended up kicking a large rock that rolled all the way down the side of the mountain. Spooky!

From here, it was just a moderate scramble plus more screen until I got to the “crux” of the climb. I hadn’t done any research on the so called crux prior to this but I assumed this was the move as there were 3 climbers lined up going down using the assisted rope. After a little bit of conversation I decided to wait for them. The formation was basically a big rock wall (the third rock band) with 2 gullys. The one on climber’s right had the rope. I looked around the wall and decided to take the gully on climber’s left. I could’ve gone down the middle too, as with the rock formations in the rockies theres always alot of holds. I wedged myself in the gully (it was more like a crack) and 2 handed my way up with the large solid holds. I got to the top (to their suprise as they were still on the rope) and continued on.

From the top, I continued along more mixed scree and rock. The grade here was better, and I stumbled my way up some rockpiles to reach some crazy viewpoints. There was another small slab that I had to go over aswell. At this point, I ate a few snacks but wasn’t feeling too well. My breathing was heavy and I was starting to feel a headache at around 3000m elevation. I suspected it was altitude sickness from pushing too fast, so I decided to slow down (I was going at blistering pace). I decided to not eat my lunch as I felt a little naseous and didn’t want to lose my rhythm going up (not a good idea)

I was still feeling like shit but at least this part was flatter. I took my time to catch my breath but was still feeling unfit and felt my headache again. I traversed along a gentle little scree field which would lead me to the bottom of an absolutely horrendous looking gravel slope.

Here is when the climb really started kicking my ass. Past the relatively tame mixed rockpiles and scattered scree, I reached a gravel slope of around 45 degrees. This would’ve been fairly straightforward if I had poles, but I didn’t… Unlike the previous larger rocks and gentle switchbacks, this route had NO TURNS and was basically a straight up to what I assumed was the summit ridge (based on alltrails elevation and what I saw with my eyes). I didn’t want to make my own path winding back and forth as any little slip would’ve been disastrous, due to the very very very loose nature of the rock. If I slipped sideways it would’ve been straight down the slope for me.

I was feeling increasingly naseous but with the summit almost in view I decided to not sit down and eat food and rest but to just continue on (very bad idea). Due to how tired and naseous I was I had to rest every half minute and basically had to crawl up for stability (had no poles). At least no one else was at this part to see me do it. Nearing the ridge, the grade eased up. No pictures were taken of the really bad parts as I had to lock in.

After alot more huffing and puffing I finally slogged my way up the last prat of the slope and made it onto the ridge. Words could not describe how relieved I felt after seeing how close the summit was. Around 8 or so people were at the summit, and I made my way there via a easy traverse, making sure to stick close to the slope and not the ledge. The North face of Mount Temple is a famous “big wall” and from here I could see why. It was very steep with significant exposure and there was still snow stuck to it even in late august. I made sure to stay away from it as I did see some cornices. I was still feeling sick, I had a really bad headache aswell as labored breathing.

After around 4 hours of climbing, I finally made it to the summit of Mount Temple. It was exciting with this mountain being my first time above 3000m (where you actually start to feel the lack of oxygen), and my first peak in the Canadian Rockies 11000ers. I was feeling very tired and my headache was just getting worse and worse. After a little bit of time standing and taking photos of the scenery, I sat down to take a little break.

After taking a little rest, I decided to walk forward to the end of the summit ridge. To my surprise, there was still alot of snow and I asked a fellow climber I met to snag a photo of me. My headache was getting quite bad, so I decided to quickly start to head down.

I decided to head back down after only about 10 minutes at the top due to concerns of my headache. I quickly walked back down the ridge and got down the scree slope with minimal difficulty. It’s ALOT easier to get down scree and you can just walk sidways and slide – versus having to climb up

After getting back down the gravel slope, it was only a short walk until I reached the crux “wall” . There were a few climbers who were going up from here. I waited for them to ascend via the rope, then went down the gully. I went down the gully with the fixed rope (not the one I went up) but I opted to skip using the rope. After this part, it was just some straightforward retracing of my steps down the mountain. I felt better and better the more elevation I descended.

One thing about the Canadian rockies is how diverse the rock quality was. In just about 1 hour of descending I saw so many different types of rocks, all shaped uniquely and with different colors depending on what elevation they were placed at. This is thanks to the sedimentary nature of the canadian rockies. It felt pretty surreal walking past all of these layers of history.

From here, I quickly made it back to the big rock pillar and traversed past it. Another hour or so of climbing and I made it back to the sentinel pass. There wasn’t as many hikers here anymore, and I opted to take a small little break before continuing down. I laid down and it was relaxing having the sun streaming onto me.

I was still feeling naseous and hungry at the same time so I decided to speed the rest of my way down. The switchback leading back into the valley was light grade so I decided to jog. I was also feeling really thirsty and had run out of water halfway before descending, so I decided to take a risk and drink some of the water flowing between the 2 lakes. It tasted okay which was a good sign.

I quickly walked through the valley, enjoying the sights and sounds of the nature around me. My headache would become really bad everytime I put my head down so I decided against doing that. On the gravelly part I saw a marmot making chirping sounds which was cool. There was also alot of ground squirrels, alot more than when I came up. Maybe they are more active in the afternoon.

I quickly made it back to the treeline switchback and started pushing my way down. I didn’t stop to rest much and just wanted to get to the bottom to take a break as quickly as I could. One hiker was following me pretty close as I was going down even though I was making very fast pace. I assume he wanted the protection of my bear spray + 2 hikers in a group to deter from the bears. I didn’t talk to him much but he seemed like a pretty nice guy.

It took maybe 30 minutes from the valley to reach Moraine lake again. The brilliant green water was even more beautiful in the afternoon from treeline. I decided to sit down near the teahouse and take a nice break. I ordered a gatorade because I was sooo thirsty but I wasn’t even able to drink all of it due to how naseous I was.

After taking a little rest, I headed back to the bus stop and waited around 10 minutes for the bus. My headache was feeling better but I still wasn’t feeling at the top of my game. I got on the bus and headed back to Lake Louise. My parents picked me up from the bus stop and it was a quick 5 minute drive back to our campsite. I took some quality time to rest. We had dinner, and then took a walk around the Bow River during sunset. The pink colors were absolutely beautiful and it was so worth it despite how tired I was. The sound of the river and it’s milky white hue also added to the whole experience.

That night, I had one of the greatest sleeps in my whole life. I was so nice being able to summit my first 11000er and I felt truly blessed to be in Banff on this vacation. I was really able to comprehend and enjoy everything around me aswell as being able to push myself and go outside my comfort zone in the mountains. While one of the easier climbs, Mount Temple introduced me to my first high altitude experience and I will 100 percent be coming back to bag more peaks. Banff truly is beautiful.
